How easy is it

Four things. That is the whole job.

There is no scene tree, no plugin, no second operator. A newscast is a list of stories with a picture on each one. Everything else happens when it is supposed to.

STEP 1

Open the show

Your stories, your presenter, your look. It opens where you left it, with the graphics already on their cues.

STEP 2

Press SPACE

The next story rolls. Its lower third, its wipe and its stinger fire on their own marks. You did one thing.

STEP 3

Pick a shot 1 to 8

Host, double box, triple, side rail, full screen. The boxes move to the new shot. The story keeps rolling inside its box.

STEP 4

Type a name, TAKE

A name and a title, one button, and it is on air in the network look. Off with one more.

And building the show is the same four moves

No timeline editing. No keyframes. Rows.

A show is a list. You fill it the way you would fill a playlist, and the pills on each row say how it plays.

BUILD 1

Drag media onto the list

A movie, a still, a website, a sound. Each one becomes a row. Drag rows to reorder. That is the rundown.

BUILD 2

Tap the pills

LOOP, CONTINUE, FADE UP, X DISSOLVE. Tap them on the row and type the seconds. No transitions panel, no curves.

BUILD 3

Drop a graphic on a story

Drag a lower third, a full screen or an OTS from its drawer onto the story it belongs to. It becomes a chip with an in and an out.

BUILD 4

Write the note

Type what the operator should do on that cue, in plain words. It shows above the picture when the cue comes up. Save. Done.

Everything is drag and drop.
Or folder responsive.

Drop a file on a row and it is a cue. Drop a folder and every file in it becomes a row, in order, with the pills already set. Drop a graphic on a story and it belongs to that story. Drop a picture on the OVERLAYS board and it is a bug. Drop a website. Drop a sound. If you can drag it, LUMOS takes it. There is no import dialog anywhere.

Record and stream

Two buttons. One is RECORD. One is GO LIVE.

Both take the same thing: the on-air picture and the show's finished sound. Never the console, never your desktop. What the audience sees is what goes to the file and to the platform.

Record

Press RECORD on the command bar. The settings come up once, in words: where to save, what to call it, how big, which file type, how many frames a second. Then START RECORDING and forget about it. The file is finished the moment you stop.

  • 480p, 720p or 1080p, with the plain advice next to each
  • .mp4 for YouTube, .mov for editing suites, .mkv to keep the raw recording
  • 24, 30 or 60 frames a second
  • Sound and picture on one clock, so an hour-long show does not drift
  • Named after the show and the date if you leave the name blank
  • One button exports the same settings as an OBS profile, for a station that already runs OBS

Go live, to any of them

Open the STREAM drawer. Every platform is a sub-drawer with its own server already filled in, a LOG IN button that opens that platform's dashboard where the stream key lives, and a box to paste the key into. Tick USE THIS PLATFORM. The big green button now reads GO LIVE and names where.

LUMOS Projection, in a real show

A stage show, set up in an afternoon

The Prince of Egypt, staged with LUMOS Projection: 111 cues, a parted sea, a plague of hail, a safe loop to sit in while the actors catch up. One operator, one laptop, one key. Here is how it was put together.

Drop the clips in order

Every cue is a row. Drag a movie or a still onto the list and it is a cue. The preview shows what is on the wall, the strip on the left shows what is next, and the big note above tells the operator what to do at this moment of the show.

  • A note per cue, in plain words, in the operator's own handwriting
  • NEXT IN CUE shows the picture that is about to go up
  • Four timelines: one master, three that follow or run alone

The note is the script

"You are now in a loop. Crash it after Hotep says RA! and music begins it." That is the whole cue sheet for that moment, written on the cue itself. The operator reads it off the top of the screen and presses SPACE when the actor says the word. Nobody needs a second monitor or a paper script.

Map it to the set

The projection mapping editor lives inside the live output. Pin the corners to the flats, warp onto a cube, a cylinder or a sphere, blend two projectors into one wide image, and watch the movie keep playing while you do it. What you see in the editor is what is on the wall.

  • Keystone, mask and edge blend per projector
  • Flat, cube, pyramid, sphere, cylinder and cone surfaces
  • Copy a map to the next projector, apply it to all
The pills

Tap a pill. That is the programming.

There is no scripting in LUMOS. A cue does what its pills say. Tap one on the row and it lights up. That is the same in Broadcaster and in Projection.

HLD FIRST

Hold on the first frame until SPACE. The picture is up, the movie waits for the actor.

SKIP

Leave this row in the list but jump past it tonight.

FADE DOWN

Fade the wall to black at the end of this cue, over the seconds you give it.

FADE UP

Come up from black into this cue instead of cutting.

X DISSOLVE

Cross-dissolve from the last picture into this one. Type the seconds on the row.

AUTO XFADE

Start dissolving into the next cue before this one ends, by itself, so a sequence flows without a press.

CONTINUE

When this cue ends, roll straight into the next one. A run of clips becomes one press.

LOOP

Play this cue round and round until SPACE. The safe place to sit while the scene runs long.

COVER

Hold the last frame on the wall while the next cue loads behind it. No black, ever.

AUTO FTB

Fade to black on its own when the cue runs out, for closers and beds.

A LOOP with a two-second X DISSOLVE, a FADE UP over six, a CONTINUE. Read straight off the rows of The Prince of Egypt.

System

What it runs on

LUMOS is native Windows code on Direct3D 11, with hardware video decode. It runs on the laptop you already own. Final numbers will be published with the release; these are the machines it is being built and shown on.

Operating system

Windows 10 64-bit (build 1909 or later) or Windows 11.

Graphics

Any DirectX 11 GPU with hardware H.264 and HEVC decode. NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 or better recommended for 4K movies and multi-projector output; Intel Iris Xe and AMD Radeon are fine for HD.

Processor and memory

A quad-core CPU and 8 GB of RAM minimum; 16 GB recommended when a show carries many large stills.

Storage

An SSD. Movies are read straight off the drive; a spinning disk will stutter.

Outputs

Broadcaster: a second display or TV, HDMI or wireless cast, plus your webcam. Projection: up to four projectors on the GPU's own outputs, or one over HDMI and three over a splitter.

Sound

Any Windows audio device. WASAPI exclusive mode for the cleanest output; the on-board jack is enough.

Network

Only for what needs it: streaming, guests in the browser, and the call-in line. A show plays with the cable unplugged.

Not required

No capture card, no render farm, no subscription to a cloud, no second computer.
